## Finding the best vet: a practical checklist

There is no single best vet for everyone. The right choice depends on your situation, how complex it is, where you are and how you like to communicate. A practice that is outstanding for one client can be a poor fit for another.

Useful signs of quality include verified registration with the right body, relevant experience with situations like yours, clear written terms, honest answers about risks, and good communication from the very first contact. Online reviews can help, but read them for patterns rather than single comments, and remember that the most visible firm is not always the most suitable.

## Checking accreditation before you choose

Accreditation means a practice or professional has met standards set by a regulator or professional body. It is worth checking both the individual's registration and any practice-level accreditation, as they cover different things.

Ask which accreditations apply, what they require and how you can verify them online. Accreditation is not a guarantee of the outcome, but it does mean there are standards, a code of conduct and a route for complaints.

iComply itself is not an accrediting body. We introduce practices that hold the registration their profession requires, and we encourage you to check it yourself.

## Weighing up accredited practices for yourself

The word best in your search is doing a lot of work, and it is worth being clear that no official body ranks veterinary practices from first to last. What counts as best depends on your animal, how far you are willing to travel and what you need from a practice, so the useful comparison is between the things you can actually check before you book.

Start with the RCVS register for each vet whose name you see, then ask whether the practice belongs to the voluntary RCVS Practice Standards Scheme and what that means for its premises, equipment and clinical processes. From there, compare practical points side by side: how each practice covers nights and weekends, whether it runs a preventive health plan and what that includes, and whether anyone there holds further qualifications in your animal's species or condition.

It also helps to visit before you commit, because a practice that suits one household may not suit yours. Pay attention to how staff handle your animal, how clearly they explain what happens next, and whether you feel able to ask questions without being rushed. That impression, alongside the register check, gives you a far better basis for a decision than any label.

### How can I check if a vet is accredited?

You can search the Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons (RCVS) register by name to confirm a vet is registered. You can also ask the practice whether it holds voluntary RCVS Practice Standards Scheme accreditation, which is inspected.

### What does 'accredited' mean for a vet practice?

In the UK, all practising vets must be registered with the RCVS. Accreditation usually refers to the voluntary RCVS Practice Standards Scheme, which inspects premises, equipment and clinical processes to ensure they meet certain standards.

### Does accredited mean the same as registered?

No. Registration with the Royal College of Veterinary Surgeons is a legal requirement for every vet practising in the UK, while accreditation usually refers to the voluntary RCVS Practice Standards Scheme, which a practice chooses to join and is inspected against. A practice can be perfectly properly registered without holding that voluntary accreditation.
