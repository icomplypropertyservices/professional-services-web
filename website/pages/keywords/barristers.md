## When you might need a barrister

People search for barristers when they need focused legal expertise for a specific dispute or hearing.

- You are representing yourself in court and want a barrister to examine your case and advise on the strengths and weaknesses.
- You need a specialist opinion on a point of law before deciding whether to take a case further.
- You have a hearing coming up and want an advocate to speak for you or to help you prepare.
- You are already working with a solicitor but need a barrister's expertise for a complex area of law.
- You want to instruct a barrister directly under the Public Access scheme for a matter that does not require a solicitor.

## Understanding how barristers work

Barristers are regulated by the Bar Standards Board in England and Wales. You can check the Barristers' Register to confirm that someone holds a current practising certificate and whether they are authorised to accept instructions directly from the public. In Scotland, the equivalent professionals are advocates, regulated through the Faculty of Advocates.

Barristers usually work from chambers, where clerks manage their diaries and fees. Many barristers can be instructed through a solicitor in the traditional way, while those registered under the Public Access scheme can be instructed directly by members of the public for suitable matters. Fees are usually agreed for a defined piece of work, so you know the cost upfront.

## How fees usually work (POA)

Barristers usually agree a fee for a defined piece of work, such as a written advice, a conference or attendance at a hearing, often negotiated through their clerk. Fees reflect seniority, specialism and the time needed. The chambers will quote you, POA.

Ask clerks to confirm the fee in writing, what it covers and what happens if the hearing runs longer or is adjourned. Under public access, you may need to handle some administrative tasks a solicitor would normally do.

## Questions to ask a barrister

Asking a few direct questions early tells you a lot about how a practice works:

- Are you authorised to accept instructions directly from the public under the Public Access scheme?
- What is your experience in the specific area of law my case involves?
- How will your fees be structured, and what will they cover?
- Will you be the person handling my case from start to finish?
- What do you need from me to prepare for the hearing or to give an opinion?
- How far in advance do I need to instruct you before a hearing?

## Comparing several barristers before you choose

Searching for barristers in the plural usually means you are weighing up more than one option rather than looking for a single name. That is a sensible approach, because barristers specialise in different areas of law, and the right fit depends on the facts of your case rather than on any general ranking.

When you compare, look at each barrister's area of practice and ask whether they have handled matters like yours. You can check the Barristers' Register to confirm a current practising certificate and whether they accept direct instructions under the Public Access scheme, and you can ask about their approach to fees for a defined piece of work.

It also helps to think about how you would work together: whether they can attend your hearing in person, whether they can prepare written advice, and how quickly they could take on your matter. Comparing on those practical points, rather than on claims of being the best, will tell you far more about who suits your situation.

## FAQs: a barrister

### Can I instruct a barrister directly without going through a solicitor?

Yes, if the barrister is registered for Public Access. You can check the Barristers' Register to see whether a barrister is authorised to accept instructions directly from the public. Not all barristers offer this, and it may not be suitable for every case.

### How do barristers' fees work?

Barristers usually agree fees for a defined piece of work, such as preparing an opinion or representing you at a hearing. The fee will depend on the complexity of the case and the barrister's experience. You should discuss and agree fees before any work begins.

### What is the difference between a barrister and a solicitor?

Solicitors often handle the day-to-day management of a case and can instruct barristers on your behalf. In some matters, you may need both.

### How do I check a barrister's credentials?

You can check the Barristers' Register, maintained by the Bar Standards Board, to confirm that a barrister holds a current practising certificate and whether they are authorised to accept direct instructions. You can also ask the barrister about their experience in your area of law.

### Should I speak to more than one barrister before deciding?

It can be helpful to speak to a few so you can compare their specialisms, how they would approach your matter and how their fees are structured. Each barrister will quote you directly, so you can judge the fit for yourself before committing to anything.

### Does choosing a more expensive barrister mean better representation?

Not necessarily. Fees usually reflect the complexity of the work and the barrister's experience in that area, so a higher fee does not mean a better outcome. What matters more is whether the barrister practises in the right area of law and understands your case.

### What if my matter needs a solicitor as well as a barrister?

Some cases are best handled by a solicitor who manages the day-to-day work and instructs a barrister for advocacy or a specialist opinion. If you are unsure, tell us what your case involves and a barrister can explain whether direct instruction under Public Access is suitable.

### What is the difference between a solicitor and a barrister?

Solicitors usually manage a case from start to finish and deal with clients day to day. Barristers specialise in advocacy and specialist advice, and are typically brought in for hearings or difficult legal questions.
