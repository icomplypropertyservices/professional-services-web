## Qualifications and registration to look for

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority (SRA), and you can check an individual or firm on the SRA's register. In Scotland solicitors are regulated by the Law Society of Scotland, and in Northern Ireland by the Law Society of Northern Ireland. Regulated firms must hold professional indemnity insurance and have a complaints procedure; if that does not resolve things, the Legal Ombudsman can look at service complaints in England and Wales.

The word "lawyer" is not protected on its own, so check that whoever handles your matter is a solicitor, a chartered legal executive (CILEx) or another authorised professional. Reserved activities such as conducting court litigation and certain probate and conveyancing work can only be carried out by authorised people.

## What a solicitor is likely to cost (POA)

Legal fees depend on the type of matter, its complexity and who does the work. Firms commonly charge hourly rates, fixed fees for defined stages, or (in some claims) conditional fee arrangements. Regulated firms in England and Wales must publish pricing information for certain services and give you clear costs information at the outset. The firm will give you a POA quote.

Ask for a written estimate and for an explanation of what is not included, such as court fees, barristers' fees or experts. Ask too whether legal expenses insurance or legal aid might cover some or all of the cost.

## Comparing several solicitors without losing your footing

Typing the plural "solicitors" often means you are at the comparing stage rather than the point of instructing someone: you may have a shortlist in mind, or several names from friends and family, and you want to weigh them up before committing. That is a sensible place to be, because legal matters rarely come with a single obvious choice of firm, and the right fit depends on the type of work, your location and how you prefer to be dealt with.

When you speak to more than one, ask each the same set of questions so the answers can be compared fairly: what area of law they handle for you, who will do the day-to-day work and whether that person is a solicitor or another authorised professional, how costs will be calculated, and how they will keep you informed. Note down what each says while it is fresh, since it is easy to mix up who told you what. A firm that explains things clearly at this stage is giving you a fair idea of how it will communicate later.

It also helps to be clear about the difference between a quote and an estimate. A fixed fee is a set price for a defined piece of work, while an hourly rate means the final bill depends on how long the matter takes, so ask which applies to you and what could push the figure up. Price on application simply means the solicitor will put a figure to your matter once they understand it, rather than quoting blind.

## Questions to ask the solicitor

Once you are introduced, these questions help you decide whether the solicitor is right for you:

- Do you specialise in this type of matter, and how much of your work is in this area?
- Are you regulated by the SRA, the Law Society of Scotland or the Law Society of Northern Ireland, and can I check you on the register?
- Who will actually handle my matter day to day, and are they a solicitor or another authorised professional?
- Can you explain your costs at the outset, including whether you charge by the hour or a fixed fee, and what could change that?
- How will you keep me updated, and who do I contact if I have a question?
- If I am unhappy with the service, what is your complaints procedure and what can I do if it is not resolved?

## Solicitors: FAQs

### How do I know a solicitor is properly regulated?

Solicitors in England and Wales can be checked on the SRA's register, while in Scotland and Northern Ireland you check with the Law Society of Scotland or the Law Society of Northern Ireland. Regulated firms must also hold professional indemnity insurance and have a complaints procedure.

### What does price on application mean for a solicitor's quote?

It means the solicitor will give you a price once they understand your matter, because legal work varies so much in scope. Ask for costs information at the start so you know how you will be charged and what could affect the final figure.

### Can any lawyer deal with court cases and conveyancing?

No. Reserved activities such as conducting court litigation and certain probate and conveyancing work can only be carried out by authorised people, so check that whoever handles your matter is a solicitor, a chartered legal executive or another authorised professional.

### What should I have ready before I speak to a solicitor?

Gather any letters, contracts, court papers or agreements connected to the matter, along with a clear summary of what has happened so far. Having these to hand helps the solicitor understand your situation and give you a useful quote.

### Should I approach several solicitors at once?

It is reasonable to speak to more than one so you can compare how they explain your matter and what they say about costs. Just be aware that some firms may charge for an initial meeting, so ask about that before you book.

### How do I compare solicitors fairly when their quotes differ?

Check that each quote covers the same scope of work and the same basis of charging, whether fixed fee or hourly. A lower figure for a narrower piece of work is not the same as a lower figure for the whole matter.

### What if my matter spans England and Scotland?

The law and the regulator differ between the two, so ask each solicitor which jurisdiction they are regulated in and whether they can act on the part of the matter that sits elsewhere. It may be that you need more than one firm involved.

### Is a solicitor the same as a lawyer?

"Lawyer" is a general word that covers solicitors, barristers, chartered legal executives and others. A solicitor is a specific regulated professional who can advise you, run your case and instruct a barrister if needed.
