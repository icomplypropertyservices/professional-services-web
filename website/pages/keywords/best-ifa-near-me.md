## Checking financial advisers are qualified and regulated

Financial advice in the UK is regulated by the Financial Conduct Authority (FCA). Check that any adviser or firm is authorised, or is an appointed representative of an authorised firm, on the FCA register. If something goes wrong you can complain to the firm, then to the Financial Ombudsman Service, and the Financial Services Compensation Scheme may protect you if an authorised firm fails.

Advisers must tell you whether their advice is independent (considering the whole market) or restricted (limited to certain products or providers), and how they charge. Chartered status from the Chartered Insurance Institute or the Personal Finance Society, and certifications such as CFP, show advanced qualifications.

## What happens after you make contact

Once you have been introduced, the adviser will usually want a first conversation to understand your situation before any advice is given. Expect questions about your pension arrangements, any savings or investments, your protection cover and what you are hoping to achieve, so it helps to have paperwork and policy details to hand.

At that first appointment, ask the adviser to explain their independent or restricted status and to set out charges in pounds rather than percentages. This is also your chance to ask about qualifications, such as Chartered status from the Chartered Insurance Institute or the Personal Finance Society, or certifications such as CFP, and to check the firm on the FCA register if you have not already done so.

Advice is not usually given at that first meeting; the adviser will normally go away and come back with recommendations and a clear explanation of costs. Take your time to read anything you are given, ask questions about anything unclear, and remember that you are free to walk away if the fit does not feel right. If something later goes wrong, you can complain to the firm and then to the Financial Ombudsman Service.

## Questions to ask the professional

Once you are introduced, these questions help you decide whether the financial adviser is right for you:

- Are you independent or restricted, and can you explain what that means for the products you recommend?
- How do you charge, and can you set out your charges in pounds before we begin?
- What qualifications do you hold, and do you have Chartered status or certifications such as CFP?
- Are you and your firm authorised by the Financial Conduct Authority, and what is your firm reference number?
- What happens if I am unhappy with the advice, and how does your complaints process work?
- Will I deal with you personally, and how often will we look at my arrangements together?

There is no single best adviser for everyone, because it depends on your circumstances and the advice you need. Compare independent status, charges in pounds and qualifications, and check the firm on the FCA register before deciding.

### What does independent mean for an IFA?

An independent financial adviser considers products from across the market when recommending pensions, investments and protection. Advisers must tell you whether their advice is independent or restricted, so ask them to explain which applies.

### What can I do if something goes wrong with the advice?

You can complain to the firm first, then to the Financial Ombudsman Service. If an authorised firm fails, the Financial Services Compensation Scheme may protect you.

### Will the first meeting cost me anything?

Charges vary between firms, so ask before you book whether there is a charge for an initial conversation and how fees are structured. The adviser should set out charges in pounds so you can decide with clear information.

### Can I bring someone with me to the first appointment?

Yes, many people find it helpful to bring a partner or family member, especially when pensions or protection are being discussed. It can also help to have someone else listen and take notes.

### What is the difference between independent and restricted advice?

Independent advisers consider products from across the market. Restricted advisers focus on certain products or providers. Both can give good advice; they must tell you which they offer.
