## What people searching for a corporate solicitor usually need

Corporate solicitors deal with the legal side of running, buying, selling and funding a company, so the reasons people look for one tend to be fairly specific.

- You are buying or selling a company and need help with heads of terms, due diligence and the sale and purchase agreement.
- You are bringing in an investor or raising money and need the investment documents and shareholder arrangements put in order.
- You want a shareholders' agreement or need to restructure shareholdings between founders or family members.
- You are a director worried about your duties, especially if the company is trading through difficult times.
- You need advice on warranties and a disclosure letter before signing a deal, so you know what you are taking on.

## Checking who will act for you

Solicitors in England and Wales are regulated by the Solicitors Regulation Authority, and you can check an individual or firm on the SRA's register. In Scotland the regulator is the Law Society of Scotland, and in Northern Ireland it is the Law Society of Northern Ireland. Regulated firms must hold professional indemnity insurance and have a complaints procedure; if a service complaint is not resolved, the Legal Ombudsman can look at it in England and Wales.

The word "lawyer" is not protected on its own, so it is worth confirming that whoever handles your matter is a solicitor, a chartered legal executive or another authorised professional. Some work, such as conducting court litigation, can only be done by authorised people. Corporate work often involves several advisers, so ask who will actually do the work and who you will deal with.

## Meeting a corporate solicitor for the first time

A first meeting about a company matter is mostly about establishing the facts and the shape of the deal. Expect questions about how the company is owned, who the shareholders and directors are, what has been agreed so far and what you want the end result to be. If you have heads of terms, a draft agreement or recent accounts, take them along.

It also helps to be clear about who the solicitor is acting for. In a company sale or investment, the company, the directors and individual shareholders can have different interests, and a solicitor may not be able to act for more than one of them. Raising this early avoids awkwardness later and lets you plan who else may need their own adviser.

Ask how the work will be priced and what is included. Corporate work is often quoted by the hour or as a fixed fee for a defined stage, and the solicitor should explain this before starting. Since you searched for a corporate solicitor near me, it is also fair to ask whether meetings can be in person, by phone or by video, and how quickly they can begin.

## How fees usually work (POA)

Legal fees depend on the type of matter, its complexity and who does the work. Firms commonly charge hourly rates, fixed fees for defined stages, or (in some claims) conditional fee arrangements. Regulated firms in England and Wales must publish pricing information for certain services and give you clear costs information at the outset. The firm will give you a POA quote.

Ask for a written estimate and for an explanation of what is not included, such as court fees, barristers' fees or experts. Ask too whether legal expenses insurance or legal aid might cover some or all of the cost.

## Red flags when choosing a corporate solicitor

Be cautious if you notice any of the following:

- Guarantees about the outcome of a dispute
- A firm or individual you cannot find on the relevant register
- No clear costs information or client care letter
- Requests to send money to an account that changes by email

## FAQs: a corporate solicitor

### Do I need a corporate solicitor who is local to me?

Much corporate work can be done remotely, but meeting in person can help with complex negotiations or sensitive shareholder matters. A solicitor near you may also be more familiar with local businesses and advisers. It is worth asking how they prefer to work before you decide.

### What is the difference between a corporate solicitor and a commercial solicitor?

Corporate solicitors usually focus on the company itself — buying and selling businesses, investment, restructures, shareholder matters and directors' duties. Commercial solicitors tend to handle trading contracts and day-to-day business agreements. Some firms do both, so explain your matter and ask who is best placed to help.

### What are heads of terms and why do they matter?

Heads of terms set out the framework of a deal before the detailed documents are drafted. They are usually not legally binding apart from certain clauses, but they shape what follows. It is sensible to have a solicitor look them over before you sign.

### What is a disclosure letter?

In a company sale, the seller gives warranties about the business and a disclosure letter sets out exceptions to them. This manages the seller's risk and tells the buyer what they are taking on. A solicitor can help you prepare or look over it so nothing important is missed.

### Can a corporate solicitor act for both the buyer and the seller?

Usually not, because the two sides have conflicting interests and a solicitor owes a duty of loyalty to their own client. Each side should have its own adviser. If you are unsure who the solicitor would be acting for, ask before any confidential information is shared.

### What should I bring to a first appointment about a company matter?

Bring anything that shows how the company is structured and what has been agreed so far, such as the articles of association, a shareholders' agreement, heads of terms or a draft contract. Recent accounts can also help the solicitor understand the business. If you are unsure, ask what they would find useful.

### Can I get legal aid?

Legal aid is still available for some criminal, family (especially where there is domestic abuse), housing and immigration matters, subject to means and merits tests. A matched firm can tell you whether you are likely to qualify.
