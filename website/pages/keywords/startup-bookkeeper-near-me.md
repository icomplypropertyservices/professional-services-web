## What to understand about bookkeeping for startups

Bookkeeping is not a regulated title in the UK, but anyone providing bookkeeping services for a fee must be supervised for anti-money-laundering purposes, either by HMRC or by a professional body. Membership of the Institute of Certified Bookkeepers (ICB) or the Association of Accounting Technicians (AAT) shows a bookkeeper has trained to a recognised standard and agreed to a code of conduct.

Good set-up saves expensive clean-ups and gives investors confidence in your records. Ask whether a bookkeeper holds professional indemnity insurance and which body supervises them, and make sure they are clear about where their work stops and an accountant's starts.

## Choosing between a local bookkeeper and an online one

Searching for a startup bookkeeper near me suggests you would value being able to meet in person, perhaps to hand over a shoebox of receipts or to talk through your plans face to face while everything is still new. A local bookkeeper can sit with you as you set up your software and categories, which many new owners find easier than working it out over a screen. That said, plenty of startups work happily with a bookkeeper further away, so it is worth deciding how much the face-to-face contact matters to you before you narrow your search.

When you compare the options you are introduced to, ask each one how they would handle the early set-up: which software they would suggest, how they would categorise your income and spending, and how often you would speak. Ask too how they would report on your cash runway, since a new business needs to know how long its funds will last. Quotes are on application, so ask each bookkeeper to set out what their quote covers and what would fall outside it, then weigh the answers against each other rather than choosing on price alone.

It also helps to be clear about the boundary between bookkeeping and accountancy from the outset. A bookkeeper keeps your day-to-day records in order, while year-end accounts and tax advice usually sit with an accountant, so ask how they would work alongside yours, or whether they can suggest how that side is handled. Check that they are supervised for anti-money-laundering purposes, either by HMRC or a professional body, and ask about professional indemnity insurance. A short first conversation along these lines will tell you a great deal about how they work.

## Warning signs to watch for

Be cautious if you notice any of the following:

- Unclear access arrangements for your bank feeds and software
- No plan for handing records back if you part ways
- No written agreement about scope and deadlines
- Claims to give tax advice they are not qualified to give

### What is cash runway reporting and why does it matter for a startup?

Cash runway reporting shows how long your current cash will last at your current spending rate. It helps you plan ahead and make informed decisions about spending and fundraising.

### Should a startup choose a bookkeeper nearby or one they never meet?

It depends on how you like to work. Meeting locally can make the initial set-up of software and categories easier to talk through, while some new businesses are happy to share records and speak by phone or video. Ask each bookkeeper how they would keep in touch and how quickly they would respond.

### What is the difference between a startup bookkeeper and an accountant?

A bookkeeper records the day-to-day money coming in and going out and keeps your records tidy, while an accountant typically handles year-end accounts and tax advice. Good bookkeepers are clear about where their work stops and an accountant's begins, so ask about this early.
