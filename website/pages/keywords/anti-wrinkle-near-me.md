## Checking aesthetic practitioners are qualified and regulated

Anti-wrinkle injections use a prescription-only medicine, so a prescriber such as a doctor, nurse prescriber, dentist or pharmacist prescriber must assess you, normally face to face. Dermal fillers are devices rather than medicines, and the rules are tightening: the government has set out plans for a licensing scheme for non-surgical cosmetic procedures in England.

Until licensing is in force, choose a clinic carefully. Practitioners on registers such as Save Face or the JCCP, and doctors and nurses registered with the GMC or NMC, offer extra reassurance. Clinics run by regulated healthcare providers may also be registered with the CQC.

## FAQs: anti-wrinkle treatment

### Do I need a prescription for anti-wrinkle injections?

Yes, anti-wrinkle injections use a prescription-only medicine, so a prescriber such as a doctor, nurse prescriber, dentist or pharmacist prescriber must assess you, normally face to face, before prescribing.

### How can I check that a clinic is safe?

Look for practitioners on registers such as Save Face or the JCCP, and doctors and nurses registered with the GMC or NMC.

### What's the difference between anti-wrinkle injections and other treatments?

Anti-wrinkle injections use a prescription-only medicine, while treatments like skin boosters, peels and good skincare work in different ways. A practitioner can assess your skin and recommend a combination that suits you.

### Will I see results straight away?

Results vary depending on the treatment and your skin, so it's best to set realistic expectations with your practitioner. They can explain what to expect and when.

### How do I know a product is genuine?

Ask the practitioner to name the product and show the packaging. Reputable clinics are happy to do so.
